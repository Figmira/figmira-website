## Commit changes

When you publish something on a website like this one, the button doesn't say "Publish." It says **Commit changes**.

I've thought about those two words more than I'd like to admit.

The first time it really meant something was the SK hynix note. I had written, in public, under my own name, that I believe a stock can climb to $780 a share by 2032. I had also written that I own it. Everything was ready. The facts were sourced, the math was shown, the risks were laid out. And I sat there with the cursor over that green button, running through every way it could go wrong.

What if I'm early? What if I'm just wrong? What if someone who actually knows this industry reads it and laughs? What if, five years from now, the stock is lower and the note is still sitting there with my name at the bottom?

None of those questions had answers. They still don't. **I clicked it anyway.**

There's a line I've heard for years. I don't know who first said it, and as far as I can tell, nobody does:

<blockquote class="fx-pull"><p>The edge of uncomfortable is where you find greatness.</p></blockquote>

I used to read that as motivation. Lately I read it as instructions. And the most important word in it isn't "greatness." It's "edge."

## The edge, not the cliff

Most advice about discomfort makes the same mistake. It treats discomfort as one big thing you either avoid or charge straight into. Get out of your comfort zone. Jump. Burn the boats.

But the people who actually study learning describe something more precise. Educators have long used a simple map with three zones, popularized by the educator Tom Senninger ([Mind Tools](https://www.mindtools.com/a0bop9z/the-learning-zone-model/)):

- **The comfort zone.** Things you already know how to do. It feels good, and you stop growing there.
- **The learning zone.** Things that are hard, a little scary, and still within reach. You're stretched, but you can think.
- **The panic zone.** Things so far beyond you that fear takes over. You don't learn much there. You mostly survive.

Psychology has an older version of the same shape. In 1908, two researchers, Robert Yerkes and John Dodson, found that pressure improved performance on hard tasks, but only up to a point. Past that point, it fell apart ([Yerkes–Dodson law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yerkes%E2%80%93Dodson_law)). Their subjects were mice, and scientists still argue about how neatly it applies to people. But the curve rings true for anyone who has ever frozen in an interview after nailing the practice run.

And when the psychologist Anders Ericsson spent his career studying how people become world-class at anything, he kept arriving at the same place. The best didn't practice the most. They practiced right at the boundary of what they could do, a little past comfortable, never so far that they broke ([Peak](https://sive.rs/book/Peak)).

So greatness isn't on the far side of fear. It lives at a specific place: the edge of the learning zone, just before the panic. That's the edge in the quote.

The problem is that **from the inside, the edge and the cliff feel almost identical.** Both make your stomach drop. Both make your mind invent reasons to wait. So most of us treat every edge like a cliff and step back to where it's comfortable, and we call that being careful.

## Comfort is the quiet risk

Comfort feels like the absence of a decision. It isn't. Every year you stay where you already know the answers, the edge drifts a little farther away, and the thing you almost tried starts to feel like something for other people.

That used to be an acceptable trade, because the world moved slowly enough for a comfortable skill set to carry you a long way. When the tools change every few months, it doesn't. **Staying comfortable isn't standing still. It's drifting backward** while everyone who's stretching moves past you.

## You can't think your way there

Here's the trap I fall into most often. When something feels uncomfortable, I don't avoid it outright. I research it. I plan it. I read one more article, watch one more video, outline one more version. It feels like progress. It looks like progress. It is not progress.

Brian Armstrong, the CEO of Coinbase, shared a lesson he says he learned from working with smart people, and it has stuck with me more than almost any advice I've heard ([Brian Armstrong on X](https://x.com/brian_armstrong/status/1990073384022020290)):

<blockquote class="fx-pull"><p>Action produces information.</p></blockquote>

His point: if you're unsure what to do, do something, even if it turns out to be the wrong thing. The action itself tells you what you should actually be doing.

That idea quietly solves the problem from the last section. You can't find the edge by thinking about it, because from a distance every edge looks like a cliff. The only way to learn where the ground actually drops off is to walk toward it and feel it under your feet. **Thinking gives you opinions about the edge. Action gives you facts.**

Overthinking is comfortable precisely because it produces no information. Nothing can go wrong in a plan.

## Every action is a question

The way I've started to think about it: every action is a question you ask the world, and the world always answers.

Publish the note, and you find out whether anyone reads it. Make the call, and the market eventually tells you whether you were right. Build the feature, and you find out it breaks on phones. None of those answers were available while I was planning. Every one showed up the moment I did something.

Most of what I've learned building Figmira came this way. The first research page I built had a flat map of stars. Looking at it is what made me want something more: a brain that grows with every note. That 3D brain only exists because the flat version was there to disappoint me. My first stock note opened with a dashboard of numbers, and reading it back showed me it buried the story; the rewrite started in a boardroom in 2002 instead. I wrote an early version of my last essay full of rocket ships and founder stories, read it again, and realized it sounded like everyone else. The rewrite became the most honest thing I've published.

Wrong actions aren't wasted. They're just answers you didn't want. And **an answer you didn't want is still worth more than a question you never asked.**

## Reading the signal

None of this means you should throw yourself at every scary thing. The edge is real, and so is the cliff. The skill is learning to tell them apart.

Here's what I've noticed. At the edge, I'm uncomfortable but still curious. I'm nervous, but I can still think clearly enough to learn from what happens. At the cliff, curiosity disappears. All I can think about is getting out. I'm not learning anything; I'm just bracing.

So discomfort isn't something to obey, and it isn't something to ignore. It's a signal to read. If you feel the stretch and you're still learning, keep going. That's the edge. If you feel nothing at all, you're too comfortable; step forward. And if the fear has crowded out all the curiosity, take a step back. Not back to comfort. Just back to the edge.

**Stepping back to the edge isn't quitting.** It's how you stay out there long enough for it to matter.

## Where I'm standing now

Right now, my edge is this studio: learning in public how to write, build, research, and make calls that can be checked later. Some of those calls will age well. Some won't. That's the price of standing at the edge instead of watching from the comfort zone.

And I keep coming back to those two words on that button. Commit changes. Not "know how it ends." Just commit to changing, and then find out what happens.
